## Introduction

The practical rule is not “never use antibiotics for respiratory symptoms”. Viral infections can resemble bacterial illnesses, and a viral infection may occasionally be followed by a bacterial complication. The right question is whether there is evidence of a susceptible bacterial infection that is likely to benefit from treatment. A clinician may use the symptom pattern, examination, risk factors and sometimes a test to decide. If an antibiotic is prescribed, take it exactly as directed; do not borrow one, share it, save leftovers or alter the course without professional advice.

## The shortest decision path

A cough, sore throat, blocked nose or fever does not by itself answer whether an antibiotic is needed. Use this sequence:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Identify the likely cause.** Colds and influenza are viral; some conditions, such as streptococcal throat infection or bacterial pneumonia, may require antibacterial treatment.
2. **Consider severity and duration.** Symptoms that are severe, worsening, unusually prolonged or accompanied by red flags deserve medical assessment.
3. **Use testing where it changes treatment.** A throat swab, rapid test, urine test, culture or imaging may sometimes help distinguish causes.
4. **Treat symptoms appropriately.** Rest, fluids and suitable symptom relief may be the main care for an uncomplicated viral infection.
5. **Use antibiotics only when prescribed for the current illness.** The medicine, dose and duration depend on the suspected bacterium, infection site and patient.
6. **Seek reassessment if the illness changes.** A later bacterial complication is possible even when the original infection was viral.

</div>

Do not use a leftover prescription as a test of whether an illness is bacterial. Improvement after taking it cannot establish that the medicine caused the recovery.

## Bacteria and viruses are different targets

Bacteria are living cells. Depending on the drug class, an antibiotic may interfere with bacterial cell-wall construction, protein synthesis, DNA replication or another process needed for bacterial growth and survival.

Viruses are not bacterial cells. They consist of genetic material surrounded by proteins and, in some cases, a lipid envelope. They reproduce by entering host cells and using cellular machinery to make more virus. The bacterial targets attacked by penicillin and many other antibiotics simply are not present.[1][4]

This is why antibiotics do not act as general “infection medicine”. Infection describes invasion by a microorganism; it does not identify which microorganism is responsible.

Different pathogens require different approaches:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

- **Bacteria:** antibiotics may work if the organism is susceptible and the medicine reaches the infection effectively.
- **Viruses:** some infections have specific antiviral medicines, while many are managed with supportive care as the immune system clears them.
- **Fungi:** antifungal medicines target fungal structures and metabolism.
- **Parasites:** treatment depends on the particular organism and life cycle.

</div>

A drug can be valuable against one class of pathogen and useless against another. A key opening a bacterial lock does not fit a viral one.

### Antibiotics do not “boost” the immune system

Another misconception is that an antibiotic gives the immune system extra strength regardless of cause. Antibiotics reduce susceptible bacteria; they do not generally strengthen immunity or help immune cells recognise a virus.

Some antibiotics have anti-inflammatory effects and are used in selected chronic conditions under specialist supervision. That does not turn them into treatments for ordinary viral colds or influenza. The indication, dose and treatment goal in those specialist uses are different.

## Why colds and flu do not respond

The common cold can be caused by many respiratory viruses. Influenza is caused by influenza viruses. Antibiotics cannot stop either virus from entering cells or reproducing.[5]

For uncomplicated colds, the illness usually improves as the immune response controls the infection. Treatment centres on comfort, hydration and monitoring for complications. A Cochrane review found no benefit from antibiotics for the common cold or acute purulent rhinitis sufficient to justify routine use, while adverse effects were more common among those receiving antibiotics.[6]

Acute bronchitis—sometimes called a chest cold—is also usually viral in otherwise healthy people. Cough can last for several weeks even after the infection begins to settle. That persistence does not by itself show that bacteria are present. A Cochrane review found at most limited average benefit from antibiotics for acute bronchitis, alongside increased adverse effects; the balance may differ in frail older people or others at higher risk who were under-represented in trials.[7]

Influenza may have specific antiviral treatment, particularly for people at higher risk or when treatment begins promptly. An antiviral is not an antibiotic, and its use depends on the virus, timing and clinical circumstances.

## Why recovery after antibiotics is misleading

A familiar story goes like this: symptoms were severe, an antibiotic was started, and improvement followed. The conclusion seems obvious—but several alternatives can explain the sequence.

### The illness was already near its turning point

People often seek treatment when symptoms are at their worst. Many self-limiting illnesses would then improve naturally. This statistical tendency is known as regression towards the mean: unusually severe symptoms are likely to be followed by less severe ones even without a new treatment.

If an antibiotic is started at the peak, natural recovery can be credited to the medicine.

### Other care began at the same time

Rest, fluids, fever relief, sleep and reassurance may all accompany the prescription. Any improvement is attributed to the most medically distinctive action—the antibiotic—even though it may not have caused the change.

### The diagnosis may have been uncertain

Clinicians sometimes prescribe empirically, meaning treatment begins before the precise organism is known. An antibiotic may be offered because a bacterial cause is plausible or the consequences of missing one are serious. If the patient improves, that does not retrospectively prove which organism was responsible.

### A bacterial complication may have been present

A respiratory virus can damage airway defences or alter local conditions, allowing a secondary bacterial infection to develop. In that situation, the antibiotic treats the bacterial complication, not the original virus.

This distinction explains why both of these statements can be true:

- antibiotics do not cure influenza;
- a person with influenza may later need antibiotics for bacterial pneumonia.

## Symptoms alone can mislead

Many viral and bacterial respiratory illnesses overlap. Fever, fatigue, swollen glands, coloured mucus, sore throat and cough are immune responses or signs of inflammation, not unique bacterial labels.

### Green mucus is not a bacterial diagnosis

Yellow or green nasal mucus can result from immune cells and enzymes during either viral or bacterial illness. Colour alone does not show that antibiotics are needed. Clinicians consider the whole pattern, including duration, severity, facial pain, initial improvement followed by worsening and other findings.[8]

### A severe sore throat may still be viral

Viruses cause most sore throats. Group A streptococcal infection is bacterial and may warrant antibiotics in selected patients, but cough, runny nose and hoarseness can point towards a viral cause. Where streptococcal infection is plausible, a clinical score or diagnostic test may help guide treatment.[9][10]

Antibiotics should not be taken simply because throat pain is intense. Conversely, a severe or rapidly worsening illness should not be dismissed as viral without appropriate assessment.

### A long cough does not automatically need antibiotics

Cough after acute bronchitis frequently lasts longer than patients expect. This mismatch between expected and normal duration can create pressure for antibiotics. Persistent cough may also have other causes, so duration should prompt reassessment where appropriate—not automatic antibacterial treatment.[7][11]

### Fever does not settle the question

Both bacteria and viruses can produce high temperatures, and some serious bacterial infections may occur without marked fever, particularly in older or immunocompromised people. Fever is one piece of clinical evidence, not a pathogen detector.

## When a bacterial infection becomes more likely

No home checklist can diagnose every respiratory illness, but certain patterns justify medical review.

A clinician may become more concerned about bacterial infection when symptoms are unusually severe, persist beyond the expected course, worsen after initial improvement, or are accompanied by localising signs such as difficulty breathing or findings consistent with pneumonia. Age, pregnancy, immune suppression, chronic disease and frailty can lower the threshold for assessment.

Examples of infections for which antibiotics may be appropriate include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

- bacterial pneumonia;
- streptococcal throat infection in selected cases;
- some bacterial sinus infections;
- urinary tract infections;
- bacterial skin infections;
- meningococcal and other forms of bacterial meningitis;
- sepsis caused by susceptible bacteria.

</div>

This list does not mean that anyone with a related symptom should self-treat. Different infections require different drugs, and local resistance patterns, allergies, pregnancy, kidney function and drug interactions affect the choice.

Antibiotics can be lifesaving when a serious bacterial infection is suspected. Stewardship does not mean delaying urgent treatment while waiting for perfect certainty. It means balancing the danger of missing bacterial disease against the harms of unnecessary exposure.

## Unnecessary treatment can cause immediate harm

An antibiotic can produce adverse effects even when it provides no benefit against the illness. Common problems include nausea, diarrhoea, abdominal discomfort, thrush and rash. More serious allergic reactions are uncommon but can be life-threatening.[1][2]

Antibiotics can also disrupt the normal community of microorganisms in the gut, skin and elsewhere. This may permit *Clostridioides difficile* to multiply and produce toxins, causing diarrhoea and potentially serious inflammation of the bowel. Antibiotic exposure is a major risk factor, although the level of risk varies by drug, duration and patient.[12]

Drug interactions matter as well. Some antibiotics affect anticoagulants, heart-rhythm medicines, seizure treatments or other drugs. Kidney and liver function may change the safe dose. This is why a medicine prescribed to one person for a previous illness is not a safe general-purpose remedy.

The relevant comparison is not “antibiotics versus doing nothing”. Appropriate viral care may include symptom management, testing, antivirals for selected infections, safety-net advice and reassessment if the condition deteriorates.

## How unnecessary use selects for resistance

Within a population of bacteria, some organisms may carry mutations or acquired genes that reduce susceptibility to a drug. Antibiotic exposure kills or suppresses susceptible bacteria more successfully, leaving resistant organisms with a competitive advantage. Those organisms can multiply and pass resistance genes to other bacteria.

The person does not become resistant; the bacteria do.[3]

Resistance can affect the individual who takes the medicine because resistant bacteria may remain in or on the body and later cause infection. It also affects others when resistant organisms spread through households, healthcare settings, food, animals or the environment.

The World Health Organization identifies antimicrobial resistance as a major global public-health threat.[3] A major analysis estimated that bacterial antimicrobial resistance was directly responsible for approximately 1.27 million deaths worldwide in 2019 and associated with substantially more, although estimates vary by pathogen, location and modelling assumptions.[13]

An unnecessary prescription is not solely responsible for this global burden. It adds selection pressure to a much larger system that includes human medicine, animal use, infection prevention, sanitation, vaccination, diagnostics and access to effective treatment.

### Resistance does not mean antibiotics suddenly stop working everywhere

Resistance is specific. A bacterial strain may resist one antibiotic while remaining susceptible to another. Rates vary between places, hospitals and patient groups. Laboratory susceptibility testing can sometimes identify which drugs are likely to work.

The consequence is often a narrower set of options, not the immediate end of all treatment. Those remaining options may be less effective, more toxic, more expensive or available only intravenously.

## Why inappropriate prescribing persists

The misconception that antibiotics treat viral illness is not simply a knowledge failure. It is reinforced by expectations, time pressure, diagnostic uncertainty and communication.

A systematic review found widespread public misunderstanding about antibiotic resistance and the illnesses antibiotics can treat.[14] Patients may interpret the absence of a prescription as a sign that the consultation was unhelpful, while clinicians may overestimate how strongly patients expect antibiotics.

Research in US outpatient care has found substantial inappropriate prescribing, particularly for respiratory conditions.[15][16] The exact proportion depends on how appropriateness is defined and what can be inferred from clinical records, but the broad problem is well established.

Prescribing can also reinforce future expectation. If a person receives an antibiotic for a self-limiting viral infection and recovers, they may request the same treatment next time. The clinician’s attempt to satisfy or reassure then becomes apparent evidence that the drug was necessary.

A better consultation makes the positive plan explicit: the likely diagnosis, expected duration, symptom relief, warning signs and conditions for reassessment. “No antibiotic” should not mean “no care”.

## Delayed prescriptions can manage uncertainty

In selected non-urgent cases, a clinician may use a delayed or back-up prescription. The patient is advised to start the antibiotic only if symptoms fail to improve within a specified period or worsen in a defined way.

Cochrane evidence indicates that delayed prescribing can substantially reduce antibiotic use compared with immediate prescribing while maintaining similar satisfaction and, for many conditions, similar clinical outcomes. No-prescription strategies use even fewer antibiotics, but delayed prescriptions may be useful when clinicians and patients are uncomfortable with uncertainty.[17]

This approach is not suitable for every patient or suspected infection. Serious bacterial disease requires prompt treatment. The delay period and trigger must be clear, and patients need safety-net advice explaining when to seek help rather than simply filling the prescription.

## Take antibiotics as prescribed—not by slogan

Advice to “always finish the course” arose partly from concern that incomplete exposure would select resistance or permit relapse. Modern discussion is more nuanced: the optimum duration differs by infection and drug, and unnecessarily long courses can increase adverse effects and selection pressure.[18]

That nuance should not be converted into permission to stop whenever symptoms improve. Feeling better does not prove that an infection has been adequately treated, and some conditions require a specific duration to prevent relapse or complications.

The safe practical rule is:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

- follow the dose, schedule and duration on the current prescription;
- ask the prescriber or pharmacist if instructions are unclear;
- seek advice if adverse effects occur;
- do not independently extend, shorten or restart the course;
- do not save leftovers for an undiagnosed future illness;
- return unused medicines through an appropriate pharmacy scheme rather than sharing them.

</div>

Guidelines increasingly recommend the shortest effective course supported for the diagnosed condition. That is a prescribing decision, not one a patient must improvise after treatment begins.

## What to ask during a consultation

A useful conversation can reduce uncertainty without turning the appointment into a demand for a particular medicine.

Questions include:

- What is the most likely cause of these symptoms?

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--benefit" markdown="1">

- What findings suggest a virus or bacterium?
- Would a test change treatment?
- What symptom relief is appropriate?
- How long should recovery normally take?
- What change would suggest a bacterial complication?
- If an antibiotic is prescribed, what benefit is expected?
- What adverse effects require advice?
- When should I return or seek urgent care?

</div>

Seeking an explanation is not the same as refusing treatment. If a clinician suspects a serious bacterial infection, timely antibiotics may be essential.

## When to seek urgent help

Symptoms requiring prompt assessment depend on age, health and the suspected illness. Urgent help is particularly important for severe difficulty breathing, blue or grey lips or skin, new confusion, collapse, a stiff neck with severe illness, a non-blanching rash, marked dehydration, or rapid deterioration. Babies, frail older people, pregnant patients and people with weakened immune systems may need earlier review.

A viral diagnosis can also change. Seek reassessment if symptoms improve and then worsen markedly, breathing becomes difficult, fever is persistent or returning, or the person appears much more unwell than expected.

The fact that antibiotics do not treat viruses must never become a reason to dismiss severe symptoms. It is a reason to identify the cause and use the right treatment.

## The useful correction

The myth says: “Antibiotics cure infections, so they may help a cold or flu.”

The accurate replacement is:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

- antibiotics treat susceptible bacteria, not viruses;
- most uncomplicated colds, influenza and acute bronchitis are viral;
- apparent improvement after an antibiotic may reflect natural recovery;
- bacterial complications sometimes require antibiotics;
- unnecessary use can cause harm and select resistant bacteria;
- the correct decision depends on diagnosis, risk and clinical course.

</div>

That account explains both why an antibiotic is often inappropriate and why it can still be lifesaving in the right case. The distinction is not between taking illness seriously and doing nothing. It is between matching treatment to the pathogen and using a powerful medicine where it cannot work.
