## Why the claim and its source come apart

Memory does not store a statement, its source and every surrounding qualification as one inseparable record. According to the **source-monitoring framework**, people retain features of an experience—its wording, meaning, emotional tone and context—and later infer where the memory came from.[3][4] That inference is usually efficient, but it can fail when contextual details are weak or similar sources become confused.

Imagine reading, “A household ingredient prevents influenza”, in an article that immediately calls the claim unsupported. The ingredient and disease form a compact, memorable association. The reasons for rejection may require a longer chain: the cited experiment involved cells rather than patients, no clinical trial showed prevention, and the person promoting it lacked relevant expertise. If the association remains while that chain becomes inaccessible, the claim can feel known without being correctly evaluated.

Experiments separating statement familiarity from source recollection illustrate the problem. Ian Begg and colleagues found that familiar statements could receive higher truth ratings when participants failed to recall the source that should have discredited them.[1] Other work indicates that readers can become more receptive to statements from low-credibility sources as time separates the content from its origin.[5][6]

This does not mean source memory always decays at a fixed, faster rate. Content and context vary greatly in memorability. A distinctive source may be remembered better than an ordinary claim, while a shocking attribution can become part of the claim itself. The narrower conclusion is that recognition can survive **without successful source retrieval**, leaving familiarity to influence judgement.

### Gist is efficient but incomplete

People often remember the gist of information rather than its precise wording. Gist memory is useful: it allows someone to retain the point of a conversation without storing every sentence. But the gist may omit logical operators and qualifications such as “not”, “unconfirmed”, “associated with but not caused by”, or “true only under these conditions”.

Research on learning from fictional stories provides a concrete example. Readers can absorb factual errors embedded in fiction and later reproduce them when answering general-knowledge questions, even though the story was never presented as a factual reference source.[7][8] Remembering the proposition is not the same as remembering its fictional origin.

A similar risk appears when headlines simplify research. The memorable residue may be “coffee prevents disease”, while the original article merely reported an observational association. Once the study design, sample and uncertainty have faded, later repetition can strengthen the shortened proposition rather than the more cautious finding.

## How warnings lose the race with familiarity

Warnings can work immediately. A visible false label, correction or credibility cue gives the reader a reason to reject a claim. The difficulty is that the warning and the claim may have different memory profiles.

In consumer research, Ian Skurnik and colleagues showed participants product claims accompanied by warnings that some were false. The warnings initially helped, but after a delay some participants—particularly older adults—misremembered warned-against claims as true.[9] The appropriate conclusion is not that warnings are useless or inevitably counterproductive. It is that a warning must remain retrievable when the claim returns.

Age can matter because source memory and associative binding often become less reliable in later adulthood, although individual differences are large and older people may compensate with knowledge and careful decision-making.[10][11] Distraction, cognitive load and low attention can create similar problems at any age: the proposition receives enough processing to seem familiar, but its status receives too little to be retained.

### Corrections face the same separation problem

Corrections often contain the original claim so readers know what is being addressed. This creates a structural challenge: the false proposition may be repeated in a short, vivid form, while its refutation is longer and less memorable. Later, the reader may recognise the proposition but fail to retrieve the correction.

The **continued influence effect** is related but not identical. In studies of reports that were later retracted, people sometimes continued using the original information when reasoning about what happened.[12][13] A retraction may remove a detail without supplying another explanation capable of filling the gap. Source-memory failure contributes when people remember the information but not that it was withdrawn; incomplete updating and preference for a coherent story can contribute as well.[14]

The feared “familiarity backfire effect”—in which any repetition during correction supposedly makes misinformation more believable—has often been overstated. Experiments designed to detect it have generally found that clear corrections reduce false belief, even when they repeat the claim.[15] Meta-analyses likewise find that debunking works overall, although effects vary with the original message, the correction and the time elapsed.[16][17]

The practical problem is therefore not “never mention the myth”. It is ensuring that the corrective status remains bound to the claim.

## The sleeper effect has limits

The classic **sleeper effect** offers a closely related idea: a persuasive message from a discredited source may gain influence over time if people retain the message but forget or detach the reason for discounting it. Early research compared identical communications attributed to high- and low-credibility sources and reported that the credibility gap could narrow after a delay.[18]

Later research showed that this effect is conditional rather than automatic. A meta-analysis found that delayed increases in persuasion were most likely when people noticed a strong discounting cue but did not permanently integrate it with the message.[19] If the source was never encoded, the message may be influential from the start; if the warning remains firmly connected to the claim, there is little reason for delayed persuasion.

This distinction prevents overgeneralisation. A rumour does not become persuasive merely because it is old. What matters is the changing accessibility of different memory components: the content, the source, the warning and any alternative explanation.

## Keeping evidential status attached

The most useful countermeasures follow directly from the mechanism. They make the source and correction easier to retrieve alongside the claim.

**Lead with the accurate account.** Starting with the fact gives readers a replacement proposition rather than allowing the myth to become the organising idea. The false claim can then be identified once, clearly labelled and explained.[20]

**Explain the error rather than adding a bare label.** “False” is a fragile tag. “This photograph was taken in another country five years earlier” supplies a concrete account that can later compete with the misleading caption. Corrections containing a coherent alternative tend to outperform simple retractions.[12][14]

**Bind the judgement to a reason.** A warning such as “unreliable source” may be forgotten because it is generic. A specific explanation—fabricated quotation, retracted paper, altered denominator or non-random sample—creates more retrieval routes.

**Repeat the correction, not only the controversy.** Subsequent coverage should restate the accurate account and its basis. Otherwise each new discussion supplies another exposure to the claim while assuming that readers remember an earlier correction.

**Preserve provenance.** Screenshots and copied text readily lose authorship, date and surrounding context. Keeping links to original documents, publication dates and correction notices reduces the need to reconstruct a source from memory.

**Ask where the claim came from.** Recognition answers “Have I encountered this?” Source checking asks the more useful questions: “Where?”, “Under what label?” and “Supported by what?” That deliberate retrieval can prevent fluency from becoming a substitute for evidence.[21]

These measures cannot make memory perfect. They can, however, change what remains memorable. A correction is most durable when it does not merely attach a temporary warning to a myth, but replaces the myth with a concise explanation whose source and reasoning can be recovered later.
