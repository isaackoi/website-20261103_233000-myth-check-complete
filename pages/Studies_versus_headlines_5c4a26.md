## Introduction

Do not stop at the abstract or press release. Look for the registered primary outcome, absolute numbers, limitations, corrections and later reviews. Research has found that exaggeration in science news is often already present in institutional press releases, particularly when causal language or human advice is stronger than the underlying study permits.[1][2] A headline need not be entirely fabricated to mislead; it may report a genuine finding while quietly enlarging what that finding means.

## The one-minute headline test

Before reading the entire paper, translate the headline into a precise claim and compare it with the study’s basic structure.

Ask six questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Who was studied?** Humans, animals, cells, survey respondents or records?
2. **What was done?** Was an intervention assigned, or were existing differences merely observed?
3. **What was compared?** Treatment versus placebo, high exposure versus low exposure, or one time period versus another?
4. **What was measured?** Illness, behaviour, survival, a questionnaire score or a laboratory marker?
5. **How large was the result?** What were the absolute numbers, not just the percentage change?
6. **How uncertain was it?** What range of effects remains compatible with the data?

</div>

Now compare the verbs. “Was associated with”, “predicted”, “reduced in a laboratory model” and “caused” are not interchangeable. If an observational study reports an association but the headline says a food “prevents” disease, the headline has already outrun the design.

A fast assessment is often possible from the paper’s title, abstract, methods summary and results table. A confident verdict requires more: the full methods, prespecified outcomes, limitations and wider evidence.

## Trace the claim back to the paper

Scientific claims commonly pass through several layers:

`study → journal article → press release → news report → headline → social post`

At each stage, qualifications can disappear. A result limited to mice may become advice for people. A reduction in a biomarker may become prevention of disease. “Could help explain” may become “scientists prove”.

Search the headline’s distinctive wording, the named institution, the lead author and the topic. A reputable report should identify the journal or link to the paper. If it does not, search Crossref or PubMed using the author and several title words.[3][4]

Once found, confirm that it is the same study. Similar papers may examine different populations or outcomes. Note:

- the full title and authors;
- journal and publication date;
- article type;
- whether the paper is a preprint or peer-reviewed publication;
- whether it has been corrected, withdrawn or retracted;
- whether the news story predates the final paper.

</div>

A preprint is a publicly available manuscript that has not yet completed a journal’s peer-review process. That does not make it false, but its status should remain visible in the headline and any summary.

### Read the press release sideways

Compare the paper with the university, journal or company press release. Research by Petroc Sumner and colleagues found that exaggerated causal claims and human advice in health news were associated with similar exaggeration in academic press releases.[1] This means that “the university said it” is not enough; institutional publicity must be checked against the research.

Look for wording shared verbatim across many news stories. Identical phrases may show that outlets relied on one press release rather than independently examining the paper. The number of articles repeating a claim is then a poor measure of evidential strength.

## Match the headline to the study design

The design determines which conclusions are available. It does not decide whether every conclusion is correct, but it sets an upper limit on defensible language.

### Randomised trials

In a randomised controlled trial, participants are assigned by chance to different interventions. Proper randomisation can balance known and unknown differences between groups, making causal inference more credible. Blinding, suitable controls, low dropout and adherence to the planned analysis also matter.[5]

A trial does not automatically justify a broad headline. Check whether:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

- allocation was genuinely random and concealed;
- the control group received placebo, usual care or another active treatment;
- participants and outcome assessors were blinded where possible;
- many participants dropped out;
- the reported outcome was prespecified;
- follow-up was long enough;
- the sample resembled the people addressed by the headline.

</div>

A small, short trial may support “the intervention changed this measure over eight weeks” without supporting “the intervention prevents long-term disease”.

### Observational studies

Cohort, case-control and cross-sectional studies observe differences that already exist. They can identify associations and generate important evidence, especially where experiments would be impractical or unethical. But the exposed and unexposed groups may differ in ways that affect the result.

If coffee drinkers have a different disease rate, possible explanations include coffee, smoking, income, occupation, sleep, diet, healthcare use or other characteristics. Statistical adjustment can reduce measured confounding, but it cannot guarantee that every relevant difference was measured correctly.

Reverse causation is another risk: an apparent exposure may be a consequence rather than a cause. People in early stages of illness might change their diet, making the new diet appear associated with disease.

A systematic review of causal language found that stronger inference often appeared in media and academic descriptions than study design alone warranted.[2] Unless additional causal methods and evidence justify it, observational findings should normally be described with terms such as “associated with” rather than “causes”, “prevents” or “protects”.

### Laboratory and animal studies

Cell and animal experiments can reveal mechanisms and justify further research. They do not by themselves establish that an intervention is safe or effective in people. Doses, metabolism, disease models and experimental conditions may differ substantially from real-world human use.

A headline saying a compound “kills cancer” may refer to cancer cells exposed directly to a high concentration in a dish. The accurate claim concerns those cells under those conditions. It is not yet a clinical treatment claim.

Check whether the headline preserves the experimental subject. “In mice”, “in cultured cells” and “in a computer model” are not disposable details.

### Surveys and experiments on behaviour

A survey can estimate self-reported beliefs or habits, but wording, sampling and non-response affect its interpretation. An online experiment may establish that one message changed an immediate questionnaire response without showing that it changed real-world behaviour.

Watch for headlines that replace a measured proxy with a larger construct. A short test score is not automatically intelligence; an intention to exercise is not exercise; willingness to share a post is not verified sharing behaviour.

## Check who was studied

Headlines often imply a wider population than the sample permits. A study of 80 university students can support conclusions about those participants under the tested conditions. It does not automatically describe children, older adults, other cultures or the national population.

Look for:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

- sample size;
- age and sex distribution;
- country and setting;
- recruitment method;
- inclusion and exclusion rules;
- baseline health or experience;
- the number who completed the study.

</div>

A large sample reduces some forms of random uncertainty but does not repair a systematically unrepresentative sample. Ten thousand volunteers recruited from one specialist website may provide a precise estimate for that unusual group while poorly representing the public.

Small samples can produce unstable estimates and exaggerated apparent effects, particularly when many outcomes or subgroups are examined.[6] The problem is not that every small study is worthless. It is that a dramatic estimate from a small sample usually deserves more uncertainty and replication than a headline conveys.

Check whether the result comes from the full sample or a subgroup. “Treatment helps patients” may actually mean that no overall benefit was found, but one age group appeared to improve. Subgroup findings are more convincing when prespecified, biologically plausible, supported by an interaction test and reproduced elsewhere.

## Check what was measured

The outcome named in the headline should match the outcome measured in the study. This simple comparison catches many exaggerations.

Researchers often use surrogate outcomes—measurements expected to stand in for results that matter directly. Blood pressure can be a useful surrogate for some cardiovascular risks; a laboratory marker may be used as an early indicator of disease activity. But improving a surrogate does not always guarantee better symptoms, survival or quality of life.

Ask:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--benefit" markdown="1">

- Was the outcome directly meaningful to people?
- Was it objectively measured or self-reported?
- Was the scale validated?
- Was the outcome selected before data collection?
- How long after the intervention was it measured?
- Did the study measure benefit, harm or both?

</div>

Composite outcomes combine several events, such as death, hospital admission and a minor test abnormality. A headline may imply that the intervention reduced the most serious event even when the composite difference was driven mainly by the least serious component. Inspect the components separately.

Researchers may also measure many outcomes and highlight whichever crosses a statistical threshold. If 20 independent tests are performed at the conventional 5 per cent threshold, chance alone can produce apparently positive results. Statistical corrections, prespecification and replication help control this risk.

## Compare relative and absolute effects

Relative percentages often sound more dramatic than the underlying numbers.

Suppose an outcome occurs in 2 of every 1,000 untreated people and 1 of every 1,000 treated people. The relative reduction is 50 per cent, but the absolute reduction is one case per 1,000 people. Both figures are mathematically correct; each answers a different question.

Look for:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

- the number of events in each group;
- risk per 100, 1,000 or 10,000 people;
- absolute risk difference;
- relative risk or odds ratio;
- the time period over which risk was measured.

</div>

Odds are not the same as risks. When an outcome is common, an odds ratio can appear more impressive than the corresponding risk ratio. A news story that translates one into the other may overstate the effect.

Health-statistics research has repeatedly shown that relative-risk framing can produce a different impression from absolute numbers.[7] A balanced account should provide both, along with the baseline risk and time horizon.

### Statistical importance is not practical importance

A large study can detect a very small difference and label it statistically significant. That does not mean the difference is large enough to matter in practice. Conversely, a smaller study may estimate an important benefit but remain too uncertain to exclude no effect.

Ask whether the reported change exceeds a clinically or practically meaningful threshold. A statistically detectable improvement of a fraction of a point on a long scale may have little noticeable value.

## Read uncertainty, not just the winner

A p-value does not measure the probability that the hypothesis is true, the probability that the result occurred “by chance”, or the size of an effect. The American Statistical Association has warned against treating a threshold such as `p < 0.05` as a bright line separating scientific truth from failure.[8]

Confidence intervals are often more informative. They show a range of effect sizes compatible with the data under the model used. A wide interval signals imprecision. If an estimated treatment benefit is 10 per cent but the interval ranges from substantial benefit to slight harm, the headline should not present a settled advantage.

“Not statistically significant” does not prove that two groups are equivalent. It may mean there was little difference, or that the study lacked enough information to distinguish an effect from noise. The principle is captured by the warning that absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.[9]

Also examine whether the paper emphasises different standards in different places. A non-significant primary outcome should not be quietly displaced by a significant secondary measure without clear qualification. Reviews of biomedical literature have documented “spin”: reporting practices that make non-significant primary results appear more favourable than they are.[10][11]

## Look for the result the researchers planned to test

Prespecification records the hypotheses, outcomes and analysis plan before results are known. It helps distinguish planned tests from patterns noticed after looking at the data.

For clinical trials, search ClinicalTrials.gov or the World Health Organization’s trial-search portal using the trial identifier, intervention or investigator.[12][13] Compare the registration with the published paper:

- Is the primary outcome the same?
- Was the follow-up period changed?
- Were outcomes added, removed or relabelled?
- Does the reported sample match the plan?
- Was the trial registered before recruitment began?

For other fields, preregistrations may appear in the Open Science Framework or a discipline-specific registry.[14] Systematic reviews may be registered in PROSPERO.[15]

A changed plan is not automatically misconduct. Recruitment problems, measurement failures or new information may require justified changes. The concern is an unexplained change that makes the result appear more favourable.

The broader preregistration movement aims to reduce selective reporting and distinguish confirmatory tests from exploratory analysis.[16] Exploratory findings remain useful, but headlines should present them as leads requiring confirmation rather than final answers.

## Inspect missing data, exclusions and comparisons

A result can change substantially depending on who remains in the analysis.

Check the participant flow:

- How many people were assessed?
- How many entered each group?
- How many completed follow-up?
- Why were participants excluded?
- Did dropout differ between groups?
- Were all randomised participants analysed in their assigned groups?

If people leave a weight-loss study because of adverse effects, analysing only those who completed it can make the programme look more successful and tolerable. Trials often use intention-to-treat analysis, which keeps participants in their assigned groups to preserve much of the benefit of randomisation.

For observational studies, examine how missing information was handled and which variables were included in adjusted models. A result that appears only under one of many plausible specifications is less secure than one that remains similar across reasonable analyses.

Check the comparison itself. “Twice as effective” is meaningless without knowing the alternative. The comparator could be no treatment, usual care, placebo or the best existing intervention.

## Check whether harms received equal attention

Headlines favour benefits. A study may report an improvement while also finding adverse effects, treatment discontinuation, cost or burden.

Inspect the adverse-events table and limitations. Ask:

- Were harms actively measured or only volunteered?
- Was the study long enough to detect delayed effects?
- Was the sample large enough to identify uncommon harms?
- Did more participants stop treatment in one group?
- Does the headline’s recommendation reflect both benefit and risk?

A trial designed to detect a common benefit may be unable to estimate a rare safety problem. “No safety signal was detected” is not equivalent to “proved safe”.

## Follow funding and conflicts without using them as a shortcut

Funding and author relationships can create incentives, but they do not determine whether a result is true. Read the funding statement, conflict-of-interest disclosures and the funder’s role in design, analysis, writing and publication.

Industry-funded research may be well conducted; publicly funded research may be weak. The useful question is whether design choices, outcome selection or reporting favour a sponsor’s interests. Compare the methods with established reporting standards.

The CONSORT statement provides a framework for reporting randomised trials, STROBE covers observational studies, and PRISMA covers systematic reviews.[5][17][18] The EQUATOR Network collects these and other reporting guidelines.[19] Failure to mention a checklist is not proof of bad research, but missing information makes independent assessment harder.

## Search for corrections and later evidence

A paper is one point in an evolving record. Search its exact title, digital object identifier and authors alongside terms such as “correction”, “retraction”, “replication”, “commentary” and “systematic review”.

Check:

- the journal’s current article page;
- PubMed and Crossref records;
- the Retraction Watch Database;
- PubPeer discussion;
- later papers citing or testing the result.[3][4][20][21]

A retraction means the paper’s findings should no longer be treated as reliable, but reasons vary from honest error to misconduct. A correction may repair a limited problem without invalidating the central result. Read the notice rather than treating every editorial update as equivalent.

Replication matters because an unusually strong first result may shrink when tested in larger or more diverse samples. The Open Science Collaboration’s large psychology project found that replication effects were generally smaller than the original effects and that fewer replication studies reached statistical significance.[22] That project does not establish a universal replication rate for all science, but it demonstrates why a single striking paper should not automatically become settled fact.

## Prefer synthesis over a lone dramatic study

When the question has been studied repeatedly, a high-quality systematic review is usually more informative than one selected paper. Systematic reviews use stated methods to search for, assess and combine relevant studies; meta-analysis is the statistical combination that some reviews perform.[23]

A review can still mislead if it omits unpublished studies, combines incompatible research or treats biased studies as equally credible. Check:

- whether the search was comprehensive;
- whether inclusion criteria were specified in advance;
- how study quality was assessed;
- whether results varied substantially between studies;
- whether small-study or publication bias was considered;
- how recent the search was.

A meta-analysis does not turn weak ingredients into strong evidence. A precise pooled estimate based on poorly designed studies remains vulnerable to their biases.

Living reviews, which are updated as evidence appears, can be especially useful in fast-changing fields. But the date of the latest search remains crucial; “published this year” does not mean “includes this year’s evidence”.

## A worked headline comparison

Imagine a headline saying:

> “Daily berries prevent memory loss.”

The paper reports an observational study of 2,000 adults who completed food questionnaires and a memory test five years later. People reporting the highest berry intake scored slightly better after statistical adjustment.

The study may genuinely support:

> “Higher self-reported berry intake was associated with slightly better later memory scores in this cohort.”

It does not, by itself, establish the headline because:

- participants were not randomly assigned to eat berries;
- people who ate more berries may differ in education, income, exercise or other diet;
- diet was self-reported;
- a test score is not identical to prevention of memory loss;
- the absolute size and confidence interval matter;
- the result may not generalise beyond the sampled population.

A later randomised trial with a suitable control, objective adherence measures and meaningful cognitive outcomes could strengthen the causal case. Several consistent trials and a rigorous systematic review would strengthen it further. The wording should grow stronger only as the evidence does.

## Common headline failures

**Association becomes causation.**
“Linked with” becomes “causes”, without an experimental or otherwise compelling causal design.

**A proxy becomes a real-world outcome.**
A biomarker change becomes prevention, recovery or longer life.

**Relative change hides the baseline.**
A large percentage describes a tiny absolute difference.

**The sample becomes everyone.**
Results from mice, students, one country or a specialist clinic are generalised without justification.

**One subgroup replaces the full result.**
An exploratory finding is presented as the study’s main conclusion.

**Statistical significance becomes importance.**
A detectable difference is described as transformative without showing its practical size.

**No significant result becomes proof of no effect.**
An underpowered or imprecise study is treated as establishing equivalence.

**A single study becomes scientific consensus.**
Earlier, conflicting and higher-quality evidence disappears.

**A preprint becomes a settled finding.**
The headline omits that peer review and correction may still follow.

**The news story quotes the authors but not the data.**
Author interpretation deserves attention, but it cannot substitute for design, results and uncertainty.

## A defensible sharing decision

Before sharing a study-based headline, use a three-level judgement.

**Supported:** The wording matches the study’s design, population, outcome, effect size and uncertainty.

**Supported with qualification:** The central finding is accurate, but the headline omits an important limitation, such as a narrow sample, surrogate outcome or uncertain estimate.

**Not supported:** The headline changes the type of claim—most often association into causation, laboratory evidence into human benefit, or an exploratory result into a confirmed conclusion.

For high-stakes health or policy claims, add a fourth question: would a reasonable person make a consequential decision based on this headline that the paper itself does not justify? If so, preserve the qualifications in the title or opening sentence rather than burying them at the end.

A strong summary does not make research sound weaker than it is. It makes the strength of the language match the strength of the evidence.
