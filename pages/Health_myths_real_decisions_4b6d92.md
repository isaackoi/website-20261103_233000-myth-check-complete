## Introduction

Three case families make the pattern clear. The discredited claim linking the measles, mumps and rubella vaccine to autism survived retraction and repeated contrary studies. Confusion about viruses and bacteria encourages antibiotic use that cannot treat the underlying infection and contributes to antimicrobial resistance. Brain myths cause less immediate physical harm, but can distort education, consumer choices and expectations of treatment. In each case, the durable myth starts with a fragment of genuine scientific language and extends it far beyond what the evidence supports.

## The vaccine-autism claim outlived its source

The modern measles, mumps and rubella vaccine scare is closely associated with a 1998 paper in *The Lancet*. The paper described 12 children and suggested that their developmental and intestinal symptoms might be connected with the vaccine. Its design could not establish that vaccination caused autism: it had no appropriate control group, relied heavily on retrospective accounts and involved a tiny, selected case series.

The paper was partially retracted in 2004 and fully retracted by *The Lancet* in 2010.[3] A detailed *BMJ* investigation by journalist Brian Deer reported discrepancies between the published descriptions and the children’s medical records, along with undisclosed conflicts surrounding the work.[4]

Retraction removed the paper from the reliable scientific record, but it could not remove the story from public memory. The claim had several qualities that make a medical myth resilient: it named a feared outcome, appeared to identify a specific trigger and offered parents an explanation for changes noticed during early childhood. The timing was emotionally persuasive even though temporal sequence alone could not establish causation.

### Larger studies found no increased autism risk

Subsequent research has examined far larger populations using designs better suited to detecting an association.

A Danish nationwide cohort study followed 657,461 children born between 1999 and 2010. It found no increased risk of autism after measles, mumps and rubella vaccination, including among subgroups thought to be at increased risk.[5] A meta-analysis covering cohort and case-control studies involving more than 1.2 million children likewise found no association between vaccination and autism or autistic spectrum disorder.[6]

Researchers have also studied children with an older sibling who has autism, a group whose families may be especially concerned about risk. A US study published in *JAMA* found no harmful association between measles, mumps and rubella vaccination and autism diagnosis in children with or without an older autistic sibling.[7]

No observational study can prove a universal negative in the abstract. The relevant conclusion is that large, appropriately designed studies have repeatedly failed to find the claimed association, while the paper that launched the scare was small, methodologically incapable of proving causation and retracted.

### Real side effects do not rescue a false claim

Vaccines, like other medical products, can cause side effects. Most are mild and temporary; rare serious reactions are monitored through clinical studies and post-authorisation surveillance.[8] Acknowledging those risks is part of accurate communication, not a concession to the autism claim.

A sound risk comparison asks:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

- What adverse event is being claimed?
- How frequently does it occur after vaccination?
- Does it occur more often than the expected background rate?
- Is there a plausible and supported causal mechanism?
- What are the risks of the disease vaccination prevents?

</div>

An adverse event reported after vaccination is not automatically caused by it. When millions of people are vaccinated, illnesses and diagnoses will occur afterwards by coincidence. Safety systems use comparison groups, observed-versus-expected analyses and clinical assessment to distinguish signals from background events.

### The public-health consequence is collective

Vaccine decisions affect more than one individual because infectious diseases spread between people. Measles is exceptionally contagious, and the World Health Organization identifies high, sustained vaccination coverage as essential to preventing outbreaks.[9]

Exposure to alarming vaccine content can shift perceived risk and willingness to vaccinate. Experiments involving vaccine-critical websites found that even brief exposure increased perceptions of vaccination risk and reduced intention to vaccinate.[10] During the COVID-19 pandemic, experimental exposure to vaccine misinformation reduced stated vaccination intentions in both the United Kingdom and United States, although intentions are not identical to actual uptake.[2]

The practical mistake is to compare a vivid hypothetical vaccine injury with an invisible alternative in which infection is prevented. A balanced decision compares known vaccine benefits and risks with the probability and consequences of remaining susceptible to disease.

## Antibiotics do not treat viral infections

Antibiotics act against bacteria through mechanisms such as disrupting bacterial cell walls, protein production or replication. Viruses have different structures and reproduce inside host cells. Antibiotics therefore do not treat the viruses responsible for colds, influenza and many sore throats, coughs and cases of acute bronchitis.[11][12]

This distinction sounds simple, but several experiences make the misconception persuasive. A person may take an antibiotic for a viral illness and recover several days later. The recovery feels like proof, even though many viral infections resolve through the immune response over the same period. A prescription may also coincide with rest, fluids and symptom relief.

Sometimes the picture is genuinely complicated. A viral infection can be followed by a bacterial complication, and similar symptoms can have different causes. The correct lesson is not that antibiotics are never appropriate for respiratory symptoms; it is that symptoms alone do not justify assuming a bacterial infection. Clinical assessment and, in some cases, diagnostic testing determine whether antibacterial treatment is likely to help.

### Why “just in case” has a cost

Taking an unnecessary antibiotic offers no benefit against the virus but can still cause diarrhoea, allergic reactions, drug interactions and other adverse effects. It also exposes bacteria to the medicine, selecting for resistant strains. Antimicrobial resistance occurs when microorganisms evolve so that medicines no longer work effectively against them; it is the organisms, not the patient, that become resistant.[13]

Resistance changes future treatment. An infection that was once straightforward may require a different medicine, intravenous treatment or hospital care. Resistant organisms can also spread to other people. The decision to use an antibiotic therefore has both immediate and population-level consequences.

A systematic review of public knowledge and expectations found persistent misunderstandings about antibiotics, including uncertainty about whether they work against viruses and expectations that clinicians should prescribe them for respiratory illness.[14] Studies of outpatient prescribing have also documented substantial inappropriate antibiotic use, although estimates depend on setting, diagnostic coding and the standard applied.[15]

### The myths run in both directions

Public-health messages sometimes replace “antibiotics cure everything” with rules that are almost as crude.

“Never take antibiotics for a cough” is unsafe because some coughs reflect bacterial pneumonia or another condition requiring treatment. “Always finish every antibiotic course no matter what” also removes clinical context. The appropriate rule is to take the medicine exactly as currently prescribed and seek advice before stopping, extending or reusing it.

Other useful distinctions are:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

- **Feeling better does not prove the original illness was bacterial.**
- **Green mucus alone does not establish that an antibiotic is required.**
- **A strong antibiotic is not necessarily a better antibiotic.** Treatment should target the likely organism and infection site.
- **Leftover tablets are not a diagnostic test.** They may be the wrong medicine, dose or duration.
- **Sharing antibiotics is unsafe.** The recipient may have contraindications, allergies or an illness requiring different care.

</div>

Antibiotic stewardship—the effort to use the right medicine only when needed, at the right dose and duration—is not simply about reducing prescriptions. Delaying effective treatment for a serious bacterial infection is also harmful. The aim is appropriate use, not the smallest possible number of prescriptions.

## Brain myths borrow the language of neuroscience

Brain myths often sound more scientific than ordinary misconceptions because they use recognisable terms: hemispheres, neurons, scans, plasticity and neurotransmitters. The technical vocabulary may be genuine while the practical conclusion is not.

These myths rarely produce one dramatic medical event. Their consequences are more dispersed: teachers may organise lessons around unsupported categories, people may interpret normal struggles as fixed neurological limits, and consumers may buy products whose claims exceed the evidence.

### “We use only ten per cent of the brain”

The ten-per-cent myth has no support in modern neuroscience. Brain imaging shows activity distributed across regions according to the task and state; neurological injury demonstrates that damage to many supposedly “unused” areas has real consequences; and the brain consumes a substantial share of the body’s energy.

The correction does not mean that every neuron is maximally active at once. Simultaneous maximum activation would not represent superior functioning. Different networks become more or less active during movement, perception, planning, sleep and rest. “Not all active at once” is very different from “90 per cent unused”.

The myth’s appeal lies partly in its promise of hidden capacity. People can learn and improve, and the brain changes with experience. Neither fact implies the existence of a vast dormant reserve waiting to be switched on by a programme, supplement or device.

### “People are left-brained or right-brained”

The brain’s hemispheres do show lateralisation: some functions depend more heavily on networks in one hemisphere than the other. Language is often left-lateralised, while aspects of spatial processing may rely more strongly on right-hemisphere systems. The misconception is the leap from specialised functions to whole-person types such as logical “left-brained” people and creative “right-brained” people.

An analysis of resting-state functional connectivity in more than 1,000 people found no evidence that individuals consistently use one entire hemisphere more strongly than the other in the manner implied by popular personality labels.[16] Complex work normally depends on coordinated networks spanning both sides.

Treating the labels as harmless metaphors can still constrain expectations. A child described as “right-brained” may be discouraged from mathematics; an adult labelled “left-brained” may see creativity as outside their nature. The underlying neuroscience does not justify those boundaries.

### “Teaching must match each learner’s style”

People have preferences for receiving information, but the popular learning-styles claim is stronger: it says that identifying someone as a visual, auditory or kinaesthetic learner and matching instruction to that category improves learning.

A major review found no adequate evidential basis for using learning-style assessments in general educational practice. To support the “matching” hypothesis, studies must show a crossover: visual learners perform better with visual teaching while auditory learners perform better with auditory teaching. The necessary pattern has not been demonstrated by suitable evidence.[17]

Surveys nevertheless find widespread belief in learning styles among educators and the public.[18][19] The idea is appealing because it recognises individual differences and seems to offer personalised teaching. But people may prefer one format without learning best from it, and the best format often depends on the material. Geography benefits from maps; pronunciation requires sound; physical technique benefits from demonstration and practice.

The useful alternative is varied, well-designed instruction suited to the content, with retrieval practice, feedback and opportunities to apply knowledge—not permanent neurological labels.

### “Brain training transfers broadly”

Practising a task usually improves performance on that task and closely related ones. The disputed claim is broad transfer: that a commercial exercise or game produces general improvements in intelligence, everyday reasoning, academic performance or resistance to cognitive decline.

A comprehensive review concluded that evidence for broad, durable transfer from commercial brain-training programmes was limited, while improvements on trained or similar tasks were easier to demonstrate.[20] Claims depend heavily on the control group, outcome selection and whether researchers measure a meaningful everyday ability rather than another laboratory task.

Neuroplasticity is real: brains change with learning, injury and experience. “Neuroplastic” does not mean that every intervention produces the advertised change or that a brief exercise can reprogramme any capacity. It describes a biological property, not a guarantee of effectiveness.

## Why brain language makes weak claims feel stronger

Neuroscience explanations can seem authoritative because they connect an everyday observation to a physical organ. Experiments have found that irrelevant neuroscience information can make poor explanations appear more satisfying to some readers.[21] Brain images can also increase the perceived credibility of an argument under some conditions, although subsequent research has found that such effects vary with design and audience.[22]

A colourful scan does not independently prove the accompanying story. Functional magnetic resonance imaging measures changes related to blood oxygenation, not thoughts directly. The image reflects data processing, statistical thresholds and comparisons chosen by researchers.

When evaluating a “brain-based” claim, ask:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--benefit" markdown="1">

- What behaviour or health outcome was actually measured?
- Does the scan add evidence, or merely illustrate a conclusion reached another way?
- Was the study conducted in humans?
- Was the comparison appropriate?
- Does the result show a group average or diagnose individuals?
- Has the claimed educational or clinical benefit been tested directly?
- Is a real neuroscientific term being used to sell an unsupported intervention?

</div>

A mechanistic story may make an intervention plausible. Effectiveness still requires trials measuring the outcome that matters.

## The common pattern behind these myths

Vaccine scares, antibiotic misconceptions and brain myths concern different sciences, yet their structures overlap.

**A vivid explanation outruns the evidence.**
A vaccination precedes a developmental diagnosis; an antibiotic precedes recovery; a brain image accompanies a claim about learning. Sequence and imagery create a persuasive causal story.

**A true fragment supports a false extension.**
Vaccines can cause side effects, antibiotics treat infections, and hemispheres have specialised functions. The myth extends the true fragment into a conclusion the evidence does not support.

**Personal experience is treated as a controlled comparison.**
“I improved after taking it” does not reveal what would have happened without it. Clinical trials and epidemiological comparisons exist because the untreated outcome cannot be observed in the same person at the same time.

**Uncertainty is presented as a hidden danger.**
No medical evidence is literally complete. Misinformation exploits that fact by treating any remaining uncertainty as equivalent to evidence for the alarming claim.

**Correction is mistaken for dismissal.**
A parent’s concern, a patient’s pain or a learner’s difficulty can be genuine even when the proposed explanation is wrong. Effective communication validates the problem without endorsing the myth.

## From online belief to clinical behaviour

Exposure alone does not determine behaviour. People interpret health information through previous experience, trust, access to care, cultural norms and advice from family or professionals. Someone may share a vaccine rumour while remaining vaccinated, or misunderstand antibiotics while accepting a clinician’s recommendation.

Even so, health misinformation can change the environment in which decisions are made. Repeated claims can make uncommon harms seem typical, create pressure on clinicians and turn routine uncertainty into suspicion. Conversely, dismissive correction can reinforce the sense that legitimate questions are unwelcome.

The most useful response combines factual clarity with a decision pathway:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

- state what the strongest evidence shows;
- distinguish known risks from unsupported claims;
- explain what symptoms or circumstances require professional assessment;
- compare the risks of acting and not acting;
- identify the appropriate official or clinical source;
- update advice when evidence changes.

</div>

For vaccines, that means comparing documented vaccine risks with the risk of infection rather than claiming that vaccines are risk-free. For antibiotics, it means explaining why the suspected cause determines treatment. For brain claims, it means asking whether an intervention improves a meaningful outcome, not whether its marketing uses legitimate neuroscience vocabulary.

## A safer way to assess a health claim

Before changing treatment, rejecting vaccination or buying a neurological intervention, identify the exact claim and the evidence needed to support it.

A strong assessment asks:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. Is the source describing a controlled study, surveillance report, case history or personal anecdote?
2. Does the evidence concern the same population, dose and outcome as the claim?
3. Is an event after treatment being confused with an event caused by treatment?
4. What are the absolute benefits and risks?
5. Is the claim supported by systematic reviews and independent replication?
6. Has the paper been corrected or retracted?
7. What are the consequences of delaying established care if the claim is wrong?

</div>

Medical knowledge changes, but that does not make every claim equally uncertain. Some conclusions—such as the lack of an association between measles, mumps and rubella vaccination and autism, or the inability of antibiotics to treat viruses—are supported by established biological understanding and extensive evidence. Responsible scepticism applies scrutiny proportionately; it does not reset well-tested questions to zero whenever an alarming post appears.
