## Introduction

This approach is called lateral reading: instead of judging a page mainly by its appearance, open other sources to investigate who produced it and how its claims compare with the wider record.[1][2] Professional fact-checkers use this habit more effectively than readers who remain on an unfamiliar website inspecting its design or “About” page.[3] Verification need not settle every dispute. Its immediate purpose is to decide whether the evidence justifies sharing, whether the claim needs qualification, or whether the safest and most accurate action is to wait.

## The quickest reliable check

For a low-stakes claim, use this five-minute sequence:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **State the claim in one sentence.** Remove emotional framing and identify what could be true or false.
2. **Search a distinctive phrase.** Put a quotation or unusual wording into a search engine to locate earlier versions.
3. **Open the original source.** Find the complete speech, study, dataset, photograph, ruling or announcement.
4. **Read laterally.** Open new tabs to investigate the publisher, author and claim elsewhere.
5. **Check date and context.** Confirm that the material concerns the same place, period, population and event.
6. **Seek independent confirmation.** Look for sources that did not simply copy one another.
7. **Decide proportionately.** Share only what the evidence supports, with uncertainty and source links preserved.

</div>

Mike Caulfield’s widely used SIFT method compresses this into four moves: stop; investigate the source; find better coverage; and trace claims, quotations and media to their original context.[1] The order matters. Do not spend ten minutes analysing a polished page if a thirty-second search reveals that it is a spoof site, an undisclosed lobbying project or a copy of an old rumour.

High-stakes claims deserve more than a quick check. Medical advice, financial allegations, election information, emergencies and accusations against identifiable people may require primary records, specialist expertise and confirmation from several independent sources.

## Define what is actually being claimed

A post can mix observation, inference, prediction and opinion so smoothly that the checkable proposition disappears. Verification begins by separating them.

Consider: “This hospital is hiding a huge rise in vaccine injuries.” It may contain several distinct assertions:

- a particular hospital recorded more incidents;
- the increase was unusually large;
- the incidents were caused by vaccination;
- officials concealed the information;
- the pattern applies beyond that hospital.

</div>

Evidence for one assertion does not establish the rest. A rise in reports is not automatically a rise in confirmed injuries; a temporal sequence does not establish causation; and a delayed publication is not by itself evidence of concealment.

Rewrite the claim in neutral language, including its implied quantity and time frame. “A hospital recorded 300 suspected reactions during June, three times the previous month’s total” is checkable. “Officials are finally being exposed” is a conclusion looking for supporting evidence.

### Identify the burden of proof

Ask what would have to be true for the claim to hold. A universal statement such as “this treatment never works” requires different evidence from “this trial found no benefit”. An accusation of deliberate fabrication requires evidence of knowledge and intent, not merely proof that a statement was inaccurate.

Also check whether the claim has shifted. Someone may begin with “this photograph is genuine”, retreat to “the event could have happened”, and then treat the second proposition as vindication of the first. Verification should follow the original claim, not a weaker substitute introduced after contrary evidence appears.

## Trace the claim to its origin

Online claims often arrive as screenshots, cropped charts, reposted videos or articles describing other articles. Each retelling can remove a date, qualification or source link. Work backwards until reaching the earliest accessible evidence.

For text, search an unusual sentence or a sequence of distinctive words. If the wording is attributed to a person, locate the full transcript, recording, court document or original publication. A quotation database, social-media graphic or news report may help locate the source, but it is not equivalent to the source itself.

For statistics, locate the dataset or report that defined the measure. Check whether a headline’s “50 per cent increase” means a rise from two cases to three, whether the figure is adjusted for population size, and whether the comparison uses consistent methods. The denominator—what the number is divided by—is often more revealing than the percentage.

For scientific findings, follow the chain to the paper or registered report. Confirm that the headline describes the measured outcome rather than a speculative implication. Search the title or digital object identifier in Crossref and the relevant scholarly database.[4][5] Then check for corrections, expressions of concern, retractions or substantive criticism, including entries in the Retraction Watch database and discussion on PubPeer.[6][7]

An origin is not automatically trustworthy. Finding it tells you what evidence actually exists and what has been added in transmission.

## Leave the page and read laterally

Traditional website checklists often encourage readers to inspect spelling, design, contact details or the presence of references. These cues are weak. Professional-looking misinformation can satisfy them, while an expert’s plain institutional page may look dated.

Lateral reading means leaving the unfamiliar page almost immediately. Search for the organisation, author, funder and claim in separate tabs. Researchers comparing professional fact-checkers with historians and university students found that fact-checkers reached sounder judgements partly because they investigated websites through outside sources rather than remaining within the sites themselves.[2][3]

Useful lateral questions include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

- Who operates or funds the publisher?
- Does it have relevant expertise or direct access to the evidence?
- How do independent sources describe it?
- Has it issued corrections?
- Does the named author exist and have relevant experience?
- Is the article reporting original work or recycling another outlet?
- Is the source selling a product or campaigning for an outcome connected to the claim?

</div>

Source investigation should inform how much weight to give the evidence, not become guilt by association. An advocacy group can publish accurate data; an established institution can make an error. The decisive question remains whether the evidence supports the claim.

Research in schools and universities suggests that lateral-reading instruction can improve online source evaluation.[8][9] It is more useful than memorising long lists of superficial warning signs because it directs attention to the network of evidence surrounding a source.

## Check evidence, context and date

Finding a real source is only halfway. Genuine material can be used misleadingly.

### Does the source say what the post says?

Read beyond the title and abstract. Look for the exact passage, figure or table that supposedly supports the claim. A news post may turn “associated with” into “caused by”, “may” into “will”, or “no statistically significant difference” into “proof of no effect”.

For studies, check:

- **Population:** Who was studied, and does the claim generalise beyond them?
- **Sample size:** Was the study large enough to estimate the effect reliably?
- **Comparison:** Was there a suitable control or reference group?

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

- **Outcome:** Did researchers measure the claimed real-world result or only a proxy?
- **Time frame:** Was the follow-up long enough?
- **Uncertainty:** How wide were the confidence intervals, and were plausible alternatives discussed?
- **Design:** Was the evidence observational, experimental, modelled or anecdotal?
- **Publication status:** Is it a preprint, peer-reviewed article, correction or retracted work?

</div>

One study rarely settles a broad scientific question. Systematic reviews assess multiple studies using explicit methods and are usually more informative than selecting whichever paper supports a preferred answer.[10] Even a review must be checked for scope, quality and currency.

### Is the material still current?

Old content regularly returns during new events. A genuine photograph of flooding in one country may be relabelled as a disaster elsewhere. A policy proposal may circulate years after it was withdrawn. A preliminary estimate may be shared after revised figures have been published.

Check four dates:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. when the underlying event occurred;
2. when the material was created;
3. when it was first published;
4. when the present post was shared.

</div>

These dates need not match. Web archives can reveal how a page appeared at an earlier time, whether a headline changed and whether a supposedly new claim existed years before.[11]

### Is context missing?

Open the complete document or recording. Read several paragraphs before and after a quotation. For video, examine what happened before the clip began and after it ended. For charts, inspect axes, units, omitted categories and the starting date.

A cropped extract can be literally authentic yet substantially misleading. Verification is therefore not only the detection of fabrication; it is the restoration of relevant context.

## Verify photographs and video

An image usually raises three separate questions: Is it authentic? Does it show what the caption says? Is it from the claimed time and place? A genuine photograph can fail the second or third test.

Start with reverse-image searching. Google Lens and TinEye can locate earlier appearances, alternative crops and higher-resolution copies.[12][13] The earliest indexed result is not necessarily the original, but it may expose an old caption or unrelated event.

Then inspect visible details:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

- road signs, shop names and number plates;
- landmarks, terrain and street layout;
- weather, shadows and vegetation;
- uniforms, vehicles and flags;
- spoken language and ambient sound;
- edits, discontinuities or mismatched reflections.

</div>

For video, extract several representative frames and reverse-search them individually. Tools developed for journalists, including the InVID verification plugin and Bellingcat’s investigation toolkit, can assist with keyframes, metadata, maps and other open-source checks.[14][15]

Metadata may provide dates, device information or coordinates, but it is supporting evidence rather than proof. Platforms often strip metadata, and fields can be edited. Its absence does not make a file fake; its presence does not establish that the caption is true.

### Treat artificial-intelligence detectors cautiously

Visual oddities can justify further checking, but they are not a reliable verdict by themselves. Compression, editing and unusual lighting can resemble synthetic artefacts, while improved generators may avoid familiar defects. Detection tools can also produce false positives and may perform differently on altered, compressed or previously unseen content.

Where available, provenance records based on the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity standard can show aspects of a file’s creation and editing history.[16] Such credentials can be useful, but missing credentials do not prove fabrication, and valid provenance does not prove that the depicted event has been described honestly. Confirm the event through external evidence.

## Find genuinely independent confirmation

Ten articles are not ten sources if all repeat the same wire report, press release or social-media post. Trace each account’s sourcing. Independent confirmation means that separate reporters, datasets, witnesses or institutions reached compatible conclusions through meaningfully separate evidence.

Look for:

- an official or primary record;
- reputable reporting with named sources or visible documentation;
- specialist analysis from someone with relevant competence;
- local reporting close to the event;
- evidence from a different method, such as satellite imagery alongside witness accounts;
- a credible source that disagrees.

Searching for disagreement is especially important. Use neutral queries rather than only the claimant’s preferred language. Add terms such as “criticism”, “limitations”, “correction”, “retraction”, “response” or the strongest plausible alternative explanation.

Fact-check databases can show whether an allegation has already been investigated. Google’s Fact Check Explorer searches structured fact-check articles, while the Duke Reporters’ Lab maintains information about fact-checking organisations.[17][18] These services are starting points, not substitutes for reading the cited evidence.

When evaluating a fact-check, ask the same questions used for any other source. Does it quote the claim accurately? Does its evidence address the whole claim? Does it distinguish falsehood from lack of evidence? Does it link to original material? The International Fact-Checking Network’s code emphasises non-partisanship, source transparency, funding disclosure, methodology and corrections as markers of accountable practice.[19]

## Use a claim-specific search strategy

Broad searches can bury the evidence beneath commentary. Build the query from the claim’s most diagnostic elements.

For a quotation, search a distinctive eight-to-twelve-word fragment in quotation marks, then repeat without quotation marks in case wording has changed. For statistics, combine the measure, institution, place and reporting period. For an image, search visible signs, landmarks and the alleged event alongside a reverse-image search.

For official records, restrict the search to the relevant institution’s domain or document type. For example, a regulation may be easier to find by searching its distinctive wording plus `PDF` or the issuing authority than by repeating a viral headline.

Search results themselves are not evidence. A result snippet may omit qualifications or pull text from an unrelated part of a page. Open the source and locate the passage.

Also remember that search ranking reflects relevance, popularity, location and optimisation—not a settled judgement of truth. Studies of media-literacy interventions suggest that teaching people concrete search and evaluation skills can improve discernment, although effects vary and can fade.[20] Verification is a habit, not a one-time inoculation.

## Decide whether to share

Verification does not always end with “true” or “false”. A more useful set of conclusions is:

- **Supported:** several credible sources and the original evidence substantiate the claim.
- **Accurate but incomplete:** the core fact is correct, but important context changes its meaning.
- **Misleading:** genuine material is framed in a way that supports an unwarranted inference.
- **Unsubstantiated:** available evidence does not establish the claim.
- **False:** reliable evidence directly contradicts it.
- **Unresolved:** credible evidence is incomplete or conflicting.

Match the wording of any share to the confidence of the evidence. “Officials have confirmed…” is different from “one witness alleges…”, and both differ from “the cause remains unknown”.

If a claim is urgent but unresolved, do not convert urgency into certainty. State what is known, what has not been verified and which authoritative sources are expected to update the record.

Accuracy prompts can make a practical difference. Experiments have found that briefly directing people’s attention to accuracy can improve the quality of news they say they would share.[21][22] The simplest intervention is a pause before reposting: “Do I know this is true, or do I merely find it plausible, surprising or useful?”

## Sharing can amplify a claim even when criticising it

A public rebuttal may introduce a rumour to people who would never otherwise have seen it. Before reposting a screenshot, ask whether correction is necessary and whether the original needs additional exposure.

For a low-reach, low-stakes rumour, withholding amplification may be best. For a claim already spreading widely or likely to cause harm, correct it without turning the falsehood into the headline. Lead with the accurate account, label the claim clearly, explain the error and link to the underlying evidence.[23]

Avoid quote-sharing the original merely to express outrage. A screenshot with an explicit falsehood label can preserve evidence without sending traffic or engagement to the source, although it should not expose private information. When correcting someone directly, address the claim rather than humiliating the person who shared it.

If you discover that your own post is wrong, correct it where the original audience can see the change. Do not quietly edit away a consequential error without explanation. State what changed and link to better evidence.

## Common verification traps

**“It looks professional.”**
Design is cheap and easily copied. Investigate the people, evidence and external reputation.

**“It has references.”**
References may be irrelevant, misquoted or circular. Open the decisive source.

**“Everyone is reporting it.”**
Map the reporting chain. Many outlets may depend on one unverified account.

**“A real expert shared it.”**
Check whether the topic falls within that person’s expertise and whether they supplied evidence.

**“There is a video, so it happened.”**
The recording may be authentic but old, edited or incorrectly located.

**“No fact-check disproves it.”**
Absence of a rebuttal is not confirmation. New, obscure and unfalsifiable claims may attract no formal review.

**“The paper was peer reviewed.”**
Peer review is quality control, not certification of permanent truth. Check design, replication, later reviews and corrections.

**“The source is biased, so the claim is false.”**
Bias affects the weight and scrutiny a source deserves; it does not logically settle the underlying fact.

**“I am only sharing to ask questions.”**
Repetition can increase reach and familiarity. If the question is genuine, send the material privately to a relevant source or frame the public post around what has been verified.

## A proportionate stopping rule

No one can investigate every post indefinitely. Use the claim’s potential harm to decide how much checking is enough.

For harmless trivia, finding the original source and one credible independent confirmation may suffice. For medical instructions, financial decisions, public safety or allegations against a person, require stronger primary evidence, relevant expertise and multiple independent checks. Do not share merely because a deadline or viral conversation creates pressure.

Stop and withhold the claim when:

- its origin cannot be identified;
- its only sources repeat one another;
- decisive context is missing;
- evidence and caption refer to different dates or places;
- qualified sources disagree and you cannot resolve why;
- the claim would cause serious harm if wrong.

“Not verified” is a legitimate result. The discipline of verification lies partly in resisting the demand to replace uncertainty with a quick verdict.
