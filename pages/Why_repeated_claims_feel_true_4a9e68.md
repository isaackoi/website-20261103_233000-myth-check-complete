## Introduction

The risk is greatest when people recognise a claim without remembering where it came from, or when many repetitions appear to signal a social consensus. A rumour seen in a headline, video, search result and family message may therefore feel independently confirmed even if every version traces back to the same unsupported source. Understanding that mechanism helps explain why familiar falsehoods can survive disclaimers—and why slowing down to recover their origin matters.

## Familiarity quietly becomes evidence

The illusory truth effect has been studied since at least 1977, when Lynn Hasher, David Goldstein and Thomas Toppino asked participants to rate statements over several sessions. Repeated statements received higher truth ratings than comparable new ones, including when the repetition occurred weeks earlier.[3] A later meta-analysis covering decades of experiments found a consistent truth effect across variations in timing, subject matter and procedure.[4]

The basic experimental design is deliberately simple. Participants first read a mixture of statements, often without being asked to evaluate them. Later, they rate old and new statements for truth. Because repetition is controlled while the statements’ evidential support remains unchanged, any difference can be attributed to prior exposure rather than newly supplied facts.

The effect is not confined to obscure trivia. Repetition can increase perceived truth even when a statement contradicts knowledge that participants could retrieve if prompted.[5][6] Nor does it require dozens of exposures. In experiments involving fabricated news headlines, a single previous encounter increased later accuracy ratings; the effect remained detectable after a week and occurred for politically concordant and discordant headlines.[7]

That finding does not mean people accept every familiar headline. Average changes in rating can be modest, and many participants still reject the claim. The practical concern is cumulative: small shifts in confidence can affect whether someone pauses, investigates, shares or dismisses a warning.

## Why easy processing feels like truth

The leading explanation centres on **processing fluency**—the subjective ease with which information enters and moves through the mind. Repetition increases fluency because the words, structure or underlying idea have already been processed. The next encounter therefore feels smoother.

Fluency is not itself irrational evidence. Accurate statements are often repeated by teachers, institutions and other people, while coherent explanations tend to be easier to understand than incoherent ones. In ordinary life, familiarity can therefore correlate with reliability. The mistake arises when the mind uses that normally useful correlation in a situation where familiarity has been manufactured or is unrelated to truth.

Experiments show that repetition is not the only route to this feeling. Statements made visually easier to read have sometimes received higher truth ratings than less fluent versions.[8] Rhyming aphorisms have also been judged more accurate than semantically equivalent non-rhyming versions, apparently because their form makes them easier to process.[9] Even a non-probative photograph—a picture that supplies no evidence for the relevant claim—can increase perceived truth by making the subject easier to imagine or bring to mind.[10]

These effects are usually subtle and dependent on context. A polished layout, rhyme or photograph cannot make an obviously impossible proposition convincing to most readers. They matter most when a claim is plausible enough that people lack a decisive answer and must rely on indirect cues.

### Familiarity can be misattributed

People experience fluency but do not always identify its source correctly. A sentence may feel familiar because it was seen in a correction, a satirical post, an unreliable advert or a list explicitly labelled false. At a later encounter, the feeling of recognition may remain while the reason for it has faded.

This is sometimes described as **familiarity misattribution**. The mind registers “I have encountered this before” but may effectively translate that into “I think I know this” or “this has probably been established”. Research separating familiarity from source recollection shows that these processes can pull judgement in different directions: remembering that a statement came from an untrustworthy source can support rejection, while recognising the statement without retrieving its source can increase acceptance.[11][12]

Fluency can also be reinterpreted. In one experiment, researchers reversed the usual truth effect by leading participants to associate familiarity with falsehood rather than truth.[13] This suggests that fluency is not a fixed truth detector. It is an ambiguous sensation whose meaning depends partly on what people believe caused it.

## Memory preserves claims better than labels

A warning and the claim it qualifies are not necessarily stored or retrieved as a single durable package. The central proposition is often simple—“product X causes condition Y”—while the qualification is more complex: the study was retracted, the image is miscaptioned, the sample was biased or the relationship was correlational rather than causal.

Over time, people may retain the proposition’s gist while losing its source and status. This is one reason warnings can disappoint. Consumer research has found that an immediate warning about false product claims can reduce belief, yet older adults in particular may later misremember the warned-against claims as true as the warning’s context becomes less accessible.[14] The result should not be simplified into “warnings always backfire”; timing, age, attention and the warning’s design all matter.

The same memory imbalance helps explain the **continued influence effect**, in which retracted information continues to shape reasoning. In classic experiments, participants read an unfolding account of a warehouse fire initially blamed on flammable materials. Even after being told those materials had not been present, they continued to use the initial explanation when answering questions about the fire.[15] A retraction was more effective when it supplied a coherent alternative explanation.

Continued influence and illusory truth are related but distinct. Continued influence concerns how an initial explanation continues to structure reasoning after correction. Illusory truth concerns how prior exposure increases perceived accuracy. Both reveal the same practical vulnerability: memory for content can outlast memory for its evidential status.[16][17]

Search results and social feeds can compound the problem. A misleading claim may appear first as an allegation, then in articles rejecting it, then in posts discussing the controversy. Later, a reader may remember that the subject and claim were repeatedly associated without remembering which sources endorsed or disproved the connection. Repetition has increased familiarity even when much of the coverage was corrective.

## When repetition looks like consensus

Repeated exposure can supply more than a feeling of cognitive ease. In social settings, it may also imply that many people independently hold the same view. Yet a single active account, syndicated article or copied message can generate numerous exposures.

An experiment by Kimberly Weaver and colleagues found that hearing the same opinion repeatedly from one person could make it seem more widely shared, even when participants knew that the repetitions came from the same speaker.[18] Familiarity was being used as a rough cue to popularity. Online, where copied posts may arrive through several channels and attribution is easily lost, the distinction between “many encounters” and “many independent sources” becomes especially important.

Platform metrics reinforce social cues. High view counts, visible shares and repeated recommendations can suggest that a claim has passed some collective test. In reality, popularity may reflect novelty, outrage, coordinated promotion or the platform’s recommendation system. A large-scale study of Twitter found that false news in its dataset travelled farther and faster than true news, with novelty and human sharing contributing to the difference.[19] That result concerns diffusion, not automatic belief: seeing a popular false claim and accepting it remain separate events.

Digital communities can also become relatively segregated by interest or ideology. Research comparing social-media platforms has found patterns of selective exposure and clustering around preferred narratives.[20] Within such environments, repetition can arrive together with approval from trusted peers. The claim feels familiar, and its familiarity appears socially validated.

This is why “I keep hearing it” is a weak substitute for “several independent, competent sources have established it”. Apparent consensus should be traced backwards:

## Emotion and identity strengthen the signal

Repetition does not operate in an emotional vacuum. Fear, anger, disgust and moral approval influence what people notice, remember and transmit. One study of political messages on Twitter found that moral-emotional wording was associated with greater diffusion within ideological networks.[21] Another series of experiments found that relying on emotion rather than deliberation increased susceptibility to fabricated news headlines.[22]

Emotion can enhance repetition in two ways. First, an emotionally striking message is more likely to be shared, producing additional exposures. Secondly, each encounter may recreate the emotional response, making the claim more mentally available. A vivid allegation can therefore become easy to retrieve even when its factual basis remains weak.

Social identity adds another layer. People use information partly to understand the world and partly to navigate relationships: whom to trust, which community they belong to, and what their group regards as obvious. Reviews of political cognition find that group identity can shape attention, evaluation and memory, especially when an issue has become a marker of allegiance.[23]

This does not mean identity makes evidence irrelevant or that people invariably believe whatever favours their side. Analytical reasoning often improves the ability to distinguish reliable from unreliable claims, and inattention can explain some low-quality sharing better than partisan motivation alone.[24][25] The narrower point is that repetition from trusted peers carries two cues at once: familiarity and belonging. Rejecting the claim may then feel like questioning the judgement of friends or allies rather than merely correcting a detached fact.

## What limits the effect

The illusory truth effect is robust, but several popular interpretations overstate it.

**Repetition is not mind control.** It normally shifts the probability or degree of belief; it does not make any statement believable. Claims that violate strong knowledge or seem implausible have less room to benefit from familiarity.[1][6]

**Knowledge still matters.** People can use stored knowledge to reject falsehoods, particularly when a task encourages them to retrieve it before relying on fluency. The important finding is that possessing knowledge does not guarantee that it will be brought to mind at the right moment.[5]

**The effect can level off.** Additional repetitions may continue to increase familiarity without producing equal increases in perceived truth. The relationship is not an endlessly rising straight line.[2][26]

**Source credibility still matters.** Remembering that a claim came from an unreliable source can counteract familiarity. The vulnerability arises when the source is never inspected, was ambiguous, or has been forgotten.[11][17]

**Repeated truth benefits too.** The mechanism is content-neutral: accurate statements also become more familiar. Reliable public information can therefore gain from clear, consistent repetition. The ethical problem is not repetition itself but repetition detached from evidence, or the use of reach as a substitute for verification.

These boundaries help avoid a myth about the myth: people are not passive containers into which repeated sentences are poured. Truth judgements emerge from competing cues. Repetition matters because it quietly increases the weight of familiarity, particularly when stronger cues are absent or difficult to retrieve.

## How to stop familiarity doing the deciding

Readers cannot eliminate fluency from judgement, nor would that be desirable. They can, however, create a pause between recognising a statement and treating it as established.

The most useful question is not “Have I heard this before?” but “Where did I hear it, and what evidence supported it?” Recovering the source converts a vague feeling of familiarity into a checkable memory. If the source cannot be identified, the feeling should carry little evidential weight.

Other practical safeguards follow directly from the research:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Count independent sources, not encounters.** Ten posts derived from one article provide one evidential chain, not ten confirmations.

2. **Search the central claim rather than its exact slogan.** Repeated wording can dominate search results. Looking for the underlying event, dataset or causal relationship makes it easier to find independent assessments.

3. **Check the original material.** A confident headline may exaggerate a cautious study, while a clipped video may omit the sentence that changes its meaning.

4. **Record the correction, not only the falsehood.** A short replacement explanation gives memory a usable account. “That is false” is weaker than “the photograph is genuine, but it was taken three years earlier in another country.”

5. **Pause before sharing familiar claims.** Experiments have found that prompting people to consider accuracy can improve the quality of the material they say they would share.[27] The pause helps restore truth as the task rather than novelty, amusement or group approval.

</div>

Communicators face a complementary challenge. Ignoring a marginal rumour may avoid giving it new reach, but silence is less useful once a claim is already widespread. At that point, effective correction usually begins with the accurate account, labels the false claim clearly, explains the error and reinforces the accurate explanation.[28] Repeating reliable information is valuable because familiarity will develop around the replacement account rather than being monopolised by the myth.

The central lesson is not to distrust familiar information. It is to recognise familiarity as a feeling, not a source. A claim becomes better supported when independent evidence accumulates; hearing the same unsupported proposition again merely makes it easier to recognise.
