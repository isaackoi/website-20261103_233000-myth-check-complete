## Introduction

Treat metadata and artificial-intelligence detectors as supporting clues, not verdicts. Metadata can be removed or altered, while detector performance can fall when media is compressed, cropped or produced by an unfamiliar generator.[3][4] The strongest conclusion usually comes from several independent signals pointing in the same direction. A file can be genuine but miscaptioned; an edited image can still document a real event; and authentic footage can be presented with a false explanation. Verification must therefore test both the media and the story attached to it.

## The fast verification workflow

For an ordinary social-media post, use this sequence before sharing:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Preserve the post.** Record its link, account name, caption, upload time and any claimed location.
2. **Define the claim.** Write down who or what the media supposedly depicts, where it happened and when.
3. **Find earlier copies.** Reverse-search the image or several video keyframes.
4. **Trace the source.** Look for the original photographer, filmer or first identifiable uploader.
5. **Test place and time.** Compare visible details with maps, street imagery, weather records, satellite images and other footage.
6. **Inspect the file cautiously.** Review edits, audio, metadata and provenance records without treating any single clue as decisive.
7. **Seek independent confirmation.** Find reporting, witnesses or records that do not all depend on the same upload.
8. **State a bounded verdict.** Use “verified”, “miscontextualised”, “altered”, “unsubstantiated” or “unresolved” rather than forcing every case into “real” or “fake”.

</div>

The process is investigative rather than cosmetic. A convincing-looking post can be false, while a blurred or heavily compressed recording can be authentic. The question is not whether the media “looks right”; it is whether its traceable history and external details support the attached claim.

## Preserve the claim before it changes

Begin by capturing what is actually being alleged. Save the page address, take a screenshot showing the account and caption, and note the time displayed. If the claim could cause serious harm, preserve the original file where lawful and safe rather than relying only on a platform-compressed copy.

Record four elements separately:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

- **Identity:** Who or what is supposedly shown?
- **Location:** Where was it allegedly recorded?
- **Time:** On what date, or during which event?
- **Action:** What does the post claim happened?

</div>

This prevents a moving target. When an image is shown to be old, its promoter may retreat from “this happened today” to “this kind of thing happens”. The broader proposition may be arguable, but it does not validate the original caption.

Do not assume that the account posting the media created it. Ask whether the uploader claims to be an eyewitness, credits another person or uses vague phrases such as “just received” and “reports say”. A social-media timestamp proves only when that particular copy appeared, not when the scene was recorded.

Preservation also matters because posts can be deleted, captions edited and usernames changed. The Internet Archive’s Wayback Machine can sometimes reveal earlier versions of ordinary web pages, although many social platforms restrict or frustrate archiving.[5] A missing archive is not evidence that a post never existed.

## Find the earliest available image

Reverse-image search compares an uploaded picture with images indexed elsewhere. Google Lens, TinEye and other engines use different indexes and matching systems, so trying more than one can uncover different results.[6][7]

Use the highest-quality version available. Then repeat the search with several variations:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

- crop away captions, borders and reaction graphics;
- search the central scene rather than the whole screenshot;
- isolate a distinctive building, vehicle or person;
- rotate or unmirror the image if it may have been flipped;
- try both the complete frame and tight crops;
- search visible words separately as text.

</div>

A successful match may reveal that a photograph labelled as today’s protest first appeared during an unrelated demonstration years earlier. It may also reveal an uncropped original whose missing edge changes the meaning of the scene.

Search results are leads, not automatic proof. The oldest indexed result may be a repost, and search engines do not index every account, messaging service or deleted page. Establish the earliest version you can document, then examine whether it identifies a creator, agency, location or event.

### Follow the attribution chain

If a news outlet credits a photographer or agency, search that name with a description of the scene. Photo-agency captions commonly include the date, location and circumstances. Compare those details with the viral caption, but also check whether the agency record refers to the exact frame rather than a visually similar photograph.

Where several sites use identical wording, they may all be copying one source. Independent publication does not necessarily mean independent verification. Follow links and credit lines backwards until the chain reaches the person who recorded the scene or an organisation that received the original file.

## Turn video into searchable images

Reverse-video search is less straightforward than reverse-image search, so divide the recording into keyframes: still images representing distinct moments or camera views. The InVID-WeVerify plugin includes tools for extracting keyframes and running image searches, while Amnesty International’s YouTube DataViewer has historically helped investigators locate upload information and thumbnails for YouTube videos.[8][9]

Choose frames containing useful features:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

- a landmark or skyline;
- a shopfront, road sign or number plate;
- a distinctive vehicle or uniform;
- an unusual stage, banner or room;
- the clearest view of a person;
- the moment before or after a suspected edit.

</div>

Avoid relying on the opening frame alone. Recycled footage is often reframed with a new title card while the useful match appears later in the clip.

Search multiple frames because one may be too blurred or generic to match. If results show a longer version, watch it from before the viral excerpt begins until after it ends. A short clip may conceal a staged setup, an explanation, an earlier provocation or the fact that a supposed explosion is part of a film production.

Audio should be checked separately. Search distinctive spoken phrases, song lyrics or announcements. Compare the sound with the visible environment: echoes, traffic and crowd reactions can reveal discontinuities. Audio may also have been replaced without changing the pictures.

## Test the claimed location

Geolocation means identifying where media was recorded by matching visible features with external geographical information. Start with large, stable features before interpreting tiny details.

Look for:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

- street patterns and junction shapes;
- mountain profiles, coastlines and river bends;
- towers, bridges, stadiums and religious buildings;
- road markings, traffic direction and sign designs;
- public-transport vehicles and route numbers;
- business names and telephone prefixes;
- utility poles, paving and street furniture;
- vegetation and terrain.

</div>

Search maps and photographs for combinations rather than isolated features. A red bus or palm tree is rarely distinctive enough. A particular junction beside a curved glass building and a railway viaduct may be.

OpenStreetMap can help identify roads, buildings and infrastructure, while Google Earth offers satellite imagery and, in some versions, historical views.[10][11] Mapillary and other street-level imagery can assist where conventional street imagery is missing or outdated.[12] Compare the arrangement and viewing angle, not merely the presence of one similar object.

A convincing geolocation should explain several features in the frame. Mark where the camera would have stood, its viewing direction and why the skyline or road layout fits. If one prominent landmark should be visible but is absent, investigate whether the camera angle, focal length, weather or construction date explains the discrepancy.

Do not expose a private person’s precise location unnecessarily. Verification can establish a city, neighbourhood or public event without publishing a home address or other sensitive coordinates.

## Test the date and time

Chronolocation—estimating when media was recorded—usually combines several imperfect clues.

First, search for earlier appearances. If the same frames were published two years before the alleged event, the current caption is wrong even if the original circumstances remain unknown.

Next, examine:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

- weather and cloud conditions;
- snow cover, flooding or vegetation;
- construction progress;
- posters, election material or temporary signs;
- vehicle models and public-transport liveries;
- clothing and seasonal light;
- shadows and the Sun’s position;
- clocks, screens or live broadcasts visible in the scene.

</div>

Historical satellite data can confirm whether smoke, flooding, snow or structural damage was visible around the claimed date. NASA Worldview provides access to global satellite imagery, while Sentinel Hub’s EO Browser allows comparison of imagery from multiple Earth-observation sources.[13][14] Satellite resolution and revisit intervals vary, and cloud cover may obscure the site.

Weather records can provide corroboration, but a broad match is weak evidence: rain in a large city may fit many days. A contradiction is often more useful. Footage showing strong sunshine cannot have been recorded at the claimed place and hour if reliable observations show darkness or a severe storm.

### Use shadows carefully

If location and camera orientation are known, the direction and approximate length of shadows can be compared with the Sun’s expected position. NOAA’s solar calculator and tools such as SunCalc can assist.[15][16]

Shadow analysis is easy to overstate. Sloping ground, wide-angle distortion, artificial lighting and inaccurate orientation can produce misleading estimates. Use it to test whether a claimed time is plausible, not to assign an exact minute from an ambiguous frame.

## Check whether editing changes the meaning

Most digital media has been processed in some way. Cropping, colour correction, compression, captions and stabilisation do not automatically make content deceptive. The relevant question is whether an edit fabricates evidence or materially changes interpretation.

Possible warning signs include:

- discontinuous movement between adjacent frames;
- objects or shadows that appear and disappear;
- repeated image regions;
- inconsistent resolution around an inserted element;
- abrupt changes in background noise;
- speech that does not match visible mouth movement;
- a reflection inconsistent with the alleged scene;
- cropping that excludes the source of an action.

These are prompts for further investigation, not proof of manipulation. Video compression, frame interpolation, rolling shutters, portrait-mode blur and automated enhancement can all create apparent anomalies.

Error-level analysis and other pixel-forensic techniques are frequently overinterpreted by non-specialists. Recompression can make genuine regions appear different, while sophisticated editing may leave no obvious visual boundary. A forensic finding is strongest when the analyst has the original file, knows its processing history and can explain alternative causes.

Whenever possible, compare the viral copy with a longer or higher-quality original. A visible “edit” may be a platform transition or lost frame rather than evidence that the event was staged.

## Read metadata as a clue, not a certificate

Digital files may contain metadata such as camera model, creation time, software history or Global Positioning System coordinates. ExifTool is a widely used utility for reading metadata across many file formats.[17]

Metadata can help answer useful questions:

- Does the claimed camera model match the file?
- Is a creation date broadly consistent with the allegation?
- Are coordinates present?
- Does the file report editing software?
- Are there embedded thumbnails or separate creation and modification dates?

But metadata has strict limitations. Social networks commonly strip it. Screenshots and downloaded copies may replace it. Device clocks may be wrong, time zones may be missing, and fields can be changed deliberately. A blank metadata record says little; a populated one requires corroboration.

“Photoshop” or another editor in a software field does not prove deceptive alteration. A photographer may have cropped or exported an authentic image. Conversely, metadata that names a plausible camera does not prove authenticity because those fields can be copied or written artificially.

Cryptographic hashes can show that two files are byte-for-byte identical. They cannot establish that the scene is genuine. Even a one-pixel change produces a different conventional hash, while visually similar files may have different hashes because of routine recompression.

## Be sceptical of artificial-intelligence detectors

Artificial-intelligence detectors estimate whether media resembles examples on which they were trained. They do not directly recover the history of a file.

Performance may decline when:

- a new generator produces unfamiliar artefacts;
- an image is cropped, resized or recompressed;
- filters or screenshots obscure detectable patterns;
- genuine media contains unusual lighting or anatomy;
- only a small or blurred face is visible;
- the detector’s training data differ from the material being tested.

Research comparing people and automated systems has found that both can make consequential errors in deepfake detection, with performance varying by example and method.[18] Studies have also shown that people can find synthetic faces highly realistic, undermining confidence in unaided visual judgement.[19] Detection results should therefore be reported probabilistically: “the tool flagged this file” is defensible; “the tool proved it fake” usually is not.

Use several forms of evidence. Search for the supposed original event, inspect provenance, compare other camera angles and ask whether reliable witnesses or organisations independently documented it. A convincing scene without any trace in an event where many cameras were present deserves scrutiny, although absence alone is not disproof.

### Provenance is more useful than pixel guessing

The Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA) has developed a technical standard for signed records about how media was created and edited.[20] Content Credentials can expose such provenance information where participating cameras, software and publishers preserve it.[21]

A valid chain can provide stronger evidence about origin and editing history than a detector’s visual guess. It still does not prove that a caption is accurate or that a photographed event was unstaged. Provenance answers questions about the file; context verification answers questions about what the file means.

Absence of credentials is also inconclusive. Most existing cameras, archives and social-media copies do not preserve a complete provenance chain. Treat credentials as positive, inspectable evidence when present—not as a requirement that all authentic media must satisfy.

## Seek corroboration beyond the file

The strongest verification joins internal clues with external evidence. Search for other photographs or videos showing the same event from different positions. Compare buildings, people, smoke movement, weather and sequence. Separate viewpoints make it harder for one altered file or false caption to control the story.

Look for:

- local journalists or emergency services;
- public transport or road-closure notices;
- event schedules and venue records;
- flight, shipping or seismic data where relevant;
- satellite imagery;
- livestream archives;
- eyewitnesses whose accounts can be located and time-checked;
- official statements supported by records rather than mere denial.

Investigative organisations such as Bellingcat publish methods for combining maps, imagery, archives and public records.[2] The *Verification Handbook* similarly emphasises source contact, provenance and corroboration during breaking news.[1] WITNESS provides guidance on preserving and verifying video intended to document human-rights abuses, where maintaining the original file and chain of custody can be particularly important.[22]

Contacting the uploader can resolve basic questions: Did they record it? Where were they standing? Can they provide the original file or adjacent footage? Ask open questions before revealing all the details you expect. A genuine witness may still be mistaken, but specific answers can be tested against the scene.

## Assign the right verdict

“Real or fake?” is often too crude. Use the narrowest conclusion the evidence supports.

- **Verified:** the media’s origin, place, time and relevant action are supported by converging evidence.
- **Authentic but miscontextualised:** the file appears genuine, but the caption gives the wrong event, date, place or identity.
- **Selectively edited:** genuine material has been cropped or cut in a way that materially alters interpretation.
- **Manipulated:** visual or audio content has been added, removed or changed.
- **Synthetic:** credible provenance or multiple forms of analysis support generation rather than camera capture.
- **Unsubstantiated:** the claim lacks enough reliable evidence.
- **Unresolved:** available clues conflict or are insufficient for a responsible conclusion.

State which part has been verified. Confirming a location does not confirm the date; confirming the date does not establish who appears in a blurred recording; establishing that a clip is authentic does not prove the poster’s explanation of what caused the event.

For high-stakes allegations, “unresolved” is often the correct stopping point. Verification is not improved by replacing uncertainty with an impressive-sounding guess.

## Common mistakes that waste time

**Staring at pixels before searching.**
A reverse search may identify an old image in seconds. Visual forensics should follow basic provenance checks.

**Searching only the complete screenshot.**
Text, frames and interface graphics can prevent matching. Crop down to the underlying image.

**Using one reverse-search engine.**
Indexes differ. Search several engines and several crops.

**Assuming the first social post is the original.**
An earlier copy may have been deleted, posted privately or uploaded on another platform.

**Trusting metadata more than context.**
Metadata can be changed or stripped. Match it against visible and external evidence.

**Treating an artificial-intelligence score as a verdict.**
Detector outputs are model-dependent and vulnerable to ordinary transformations.

**Finding the right place but ignoring the date.**
The same street may host protests, fires or military vehicles at different times.

**Confirming a genuine image but not its caption.**
Authentic media is one of the most effective vehicles for false context because it already looks convincing.

**Publishing sensitive details unnecessarily.**
Verification does not require exposing a victim, witness or private residence.

## Before sharing your conclusion

If the media is false or miscaptioned, lead with the accurate account rather than reproducing the sensational claim as the headline. Show only enough of the viral content for readers to recognise it, label it clearly and link to the original evidence used in verification.

If the claim remains unresolved, say so. Avoid “probably fake” unless you can explain the basis and its limitations. Preserve the distinction between “I found no earlier version” and “no earlier version exists”.

A concise verification note should answer:

- What does the post claim?
- What is the earliest version located?
- Which place and date clues were confirmed?
- What remains uncertain?
- What verdict follows from those limits?

The decisive habit is to reconstruct the media’s history rather than trusting its surface. Reverse search, keyframes, geolocation, chronology, metadata and detector scores each contribute pieces. None replaces the need to see whether the pieces form one coherent account.
